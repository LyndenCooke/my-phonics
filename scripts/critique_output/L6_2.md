### 2. Narrative critique
- The storyline is clear and touching: a girl notices a lost boy, makes a plan, gets help, waits, comforts him, and reunites him with his parents. The text alone does tell the story.
- Image–text match is generally strong (lanterns, stalls, bridge/arch, guard, reunion). However, references to “Li River,” “tangyuan,” and specific bridge wording create decoding issues without adding essential meaning.
- Emotional arc works very well. Repetition of able/ible words (“capable, sensible, responsible, possible, adorable, incredible, remarkable”) supports the book’s focus and theme without feeling like a drill.
- Some sentences run long and contain punctuation the target readers don’t need (em‑dashes, colon splices). Simplifying will aid fluency.

### 3. Level appropriateness
- Current text often exceeds the 10–15 words per sentence target and uses several structures likely to trip readers (‑sion/‑ssion, irregular vowel spellings).
- It does foreground the focus patterns (-able/-ible) well.
- To match L6.2 expectations, the rewrite keeps 5–6 sentences per page, 10–15 words each, with plentiful earlier graphemes mixed in, while still using the new focus endings naturally.

### 4. Proposed rewrite
Note: British English, only cumulative graphemes and tricky words. Able/ible foregrounded.

Page 1
- The Lantern Festival filled the river park with warm, golden light.  
- Lines of red lanterns drifted over the water and the stalls.  
- Dark rock hills rose behind the city like big, black shapes.  
- A girl looked at a paper lantern float up, then saw something terrible.  
- A small boy sat on a stone step, hugging a panda.  
- People went past as if he were invisible, and she stopped.

Page 2
- She knelt by the boy and spoke in a soft voice.  
- He looked about three, and he was shaking and very sad.  
- “Where is your family?” she asked, but he could only sob.  
- It was clear he was not capable of finding them alone.  
- She looked round at the crowd and started to make a plan.

Page 3
- She took a small notebook from her pocket and thought hard.  
- Then she wrote a clear, readable note in neat handwriting.  
- It said, “Lost child: dark jacket, green buttons, age three, soft panda.”  
- “He is by the big red lantern near the stone arch.”  
- She held the note up high and asked people to read it.  
- It was a sensible, responsible plan, and it felt possible.

Page 4
- She took the boy’s hand and went towards the lantern stalls.  
- An old woman selling rice balls read the note and sighed.  
- She pointed along the bank and sent them on to the arch.  
- Some older kids read the note and looked all around.  
- No one knew the child, and no one was able to help.  
- She did not give up, as that was not sensible or responsible.

Page 5
- Near the gate, she saw a park warden in a yellow jacket.  
- She went right up to him, held out the note, and spoke.  
- “This child is lost. He has been here for some time.”  
- The warden read the note, nodded, and spoke into his radio.  
- “Stay by the red lantern at the stone arch,” he said.  
- She sat with the boy and told a soft, kind story.

Page 6
- As they waited, she bought him a warm, sweet potato from a cart.  
- His face changed from miserable to curious, and then to almost cheerful.  
- He held the potato in both hands and looked up.  
- “Panda,” he said, and he held the toy out to her.  
- It was adorable, and she at once understood what he meant.  
- She took the panda with care, and he gave a smile.

Page 7
- The warden came back, and a woman and a man ran with him.  
- The little boy looked up, and then he was overjoyed.  
- “Mama!” he cried, as the woman rushed in and hugged him.  
- The man shook the warden’s hand and said thanks again and again.  
- He also gave the girl a long, grateful look and a nod.  
- She stood back a little, glad to see such an incredible hug.

Page 8
- The mother turned to the girl, still holding her son very tight.  
- “You kept him safe, you stayed with him, and you helped.”  
- She paused, then said a line the girl would always remember: “You are remarkable.”  
- The girl went back through the festival, past red lanterns and dark rock hills.  
- She felt warm and capable after a night that had been incredible.  
- When something terrible is visible, a responsible person does not walk past.

- I avoided -sion/‑ssion and “tch” items that weren’t on the cumulative list.

- Sentence lengths were tuned to 10–15 words. If you need the total word count strictly under 500, remove one sentence from pp.1 or pp.7 and trim pp.3 slightly; the story still reads cleanly.
