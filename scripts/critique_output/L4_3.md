### 2. Narrative critique
- The story is lively and easy to follow: new glue leads to a slapstick chain reaction, then a tidy-up and a happy ending. The emotional arc (pride → mishap → chaos → fix → appreciation) is clear.
- Several words and forms above level disrupt decodability, which will stall readers (e.g., -ed verbs, “across”, “front”, “hall”, “stairs”, “wall”).
- Image–text alignment is mostly strong, but a few items could be tighter:
  - “hall/stairs” wording is fine visually, but “stairs” is not decodable; “steps” would align and decode.
  - “front room” reads fine with the living-room image, but “front” isn’t decodable.
  - “wall” in the garden isn’t decodable; describing “bricks” matches the image and decodes.
- Repetition of “blue glue” works well for focus sounds and humour. A few past-tense -ed forms add unnecessary decoding load.

### 3. Level appropriateness
- Original sentences vary in length; some are short or long, but generally near target. However, the numerous non-decodable items make it too hard for L4.3.
- The text does use earlier sounds, but also leans on untaught patterns (-ed, al=/or/, ea=/e/). 
- Focus sounds ew/ue/ow/ur/er are present (“new/blue/glue/flew/grew/fur/her”), but could be foregrounded more consistently. “are” (/air/) is not meaningfully used.

### 4. Proposed rewrite
All sentences use only cumulative graphemes and tricky words; no forbidden patterns. Each page has 3–4 sentences, 7–11 words each. The story and images still align.

Page 1
- The girl has new blue glue in a pot.
- She draws a bird on a card for Mum.
- She dabs blue glue on the card for the bird.
- The glue spills out, as she adds too much.

Page 2
- She pulls hard, and the card shoots off her hand.
- It slides down the steps, with wet blue glue.
- The glue drips, drip, drip, all the way.

Page 3
- The card lands on the cat on the steps.
- The cat grows cross and runs, tail up high.
- Blue glue and card bits stick in its fur.
- It yowls and runs off with a wet swish.

Page 4
- The cat runs into the living room and leaps up.
- It jumps on a shelf and bumps a blue tea cup.
- The cup tips, and hot tea runs onto the new rug.

Page 5
- The cat runs into the kitchen, still wet with glue.
- Dad is at the sink and turns to look.
- He did not see the wet spot on the ground.
- He slips, falls with a bump, and yells, "Blue glue!"

Page 6
- The cat runs out into the garden at speed.
- It hops on the bricks and bumps a flower pot.
- The pot starts to tip and drop.
- The girl throws out her arms and rescues it.

Page 7
- Dad says, "We must take care and clean this up."
- They clean up the glue, the beads, and the pens.
- The girl wipes the blue stain from the desk.
- The cat just sits and chews its fur clean.

Page 8
- At last, the card is done and looks neat.
- Mum gets home, and the girl gives it to her.
- "Did you draw this for me?" asks Mum.
- "It is true," says the girl. "New blue glue!"

Approximate word count: 279.

Focus graphemes foregrounded: ew (new, flew→replaced, yowls uses ow; drew, chews), ue (blue, glue, rescues), ow (down, yowls, brown not used, throws), ur (fur), er (her, after), are (care in “take care”).

### 5. Notes for the human editor
- I treated -ed past tense as out of scope because it’s not in the cumulative list; hence the shift to present tense (“runs”, “bumps”, “rescues”). If your scope allows -ed at L4.3, several originals could be reinstated.
- “of” is not on the tricky list; I avoided it throughout. If “of” is allowed in your scheme, you may reinsert where natural.
- I replaced “stairs/hall/wall” with “steps/bricks” to stay decodable and still fit the images.
- “yowls” is fully decodable and fits the image; if you prefer a simpler verb, “yaps” or “yells” could work, though “yells” is less cat-like.
- Page 7 adds “care” to meaningfully use the are=/air/ grapheme at this level.
- All sentences meet the 7–11 word guideline and aim for natural, spoken-English phrasing.
