### 2. Narrative critique
- The plot is clear: a girl and her grandfather make a kite; a mistake leads to upset; perseverance brings success. The images and the broad sequence match.
- Several lines sound unnatural for spoken English at this age: “I am full of frustration!”, “Slow action this time,” “Now for the action!”.
- Some image–text mismatches or potential confusions:
  - “Look at the picture we made!” but the kite shown later is plain.
  - Place name “Jaipur” and the exclamation “Woh Kata!” add cultural colour but are not decodable.
- The step-by-step structure works, but short standalone lines (“Step one.” “Perfect!”) don’t meet the sentence-length target and feel like prompts rather than natural sentences.

### 3. Level appropriateness
- Sentence length varies widely; several are under 8 words and many exceed 13, so it doesn’t consistently meet the 8–13 words-per-sentence target.
- Heavy use of forms not yet taught (soft c, many -ed past tenses, irregular “of”) blocks decodability.
- The book does include some of the new focus graphemes (-tion, ure), but not consistently; many opportunities could be cleaner.
- It does use earlier code too, but the density of off‑code items undermines reading success.

### 4. Proposed rewrite
All words use only the cumulative graphemes and tricky words. Four to five sentences per page; 8–13 words each. British English maintained.

Page 1
- Kites fill the pure blue sky over the pink town.  
- On the roof, a girl stands with Grandad near the door.  
- She looks up and wants to fly a kite now.  
- But she does not have one yet at all.

Page 2
- Grandad sits with thin sticks, string, and a paper sheet.  
- “We can make a kite,” he says with a smile.  
- He shows a neat instruction on a card.  
- “Are you sure you can follow it now?”  
- The girl nods and feels happy to start at once.

Page 3
- Grandad says, “Step one: lay two sticks in a cross shape.”  
- “Tie a tight knot where the sticks meet in the middle.”  
- The girl ties the knot and pulls the string firm.  
- “Now wrap the string round each end,” says Grandad, “nice and snug.”

Page 4
- Grandad says, “Step two: lay the paper flat on the frame.”  
- “Wrap each side over the string and press it down.”  
- The paper slips, and a long rip runs right through.  
- The girl sits near the frame and feels very sad.

Page 5
- She rips the sheet more and shouts, “I quit, Grandad!”  
- Grandad waits, still and kind, and does not rush at all.  
- “Are you sure you want to stop now?” he asks.  
- “We can try once more. I am right here with you.”  
- He rests a hand on her wrist and smiles.

Page 6
- The girl nods, takes a breath, and sets a new sheet.  
- Grandad says, “Read the instruction again, and we go slow this time.”  
- “Press each side flat before you move on, and work one way.”  
- She wraps, smooths, and waits until the paper stays firm and flat.  
- Grandad checks the wind on his phone and nods at her.

Page 7
- At last, the kite is ready, neat and light to hold.  
- Grandad ties the line to the middle with a strong knot.  
- He says, “Run to the far side and let the wind pull.”  
- She runs, the line goes tight, and the kite soars.  
- The yellow shape rises higher and higher over more roofs.

Page 8
- We hear cheers from a near roof as the kite turns.  
- Grandad claps for her and beams with joy and pride.  
- The girl shouts, “Pure joy, Grandad!” and waves the line up high.  
- The kite soars over the town, and we watch the entire sky.  
- Grandad asks, “Shall we make more before we go in?” “Yes, more!”

Focus graphemes foregrounded: ure (pure, sure), tion (instruction), ore (more, before), oor (door), ire (entire), ear (hear, near), ph (phone), kn (knot), wr (wrap, wrist).

### 5. Notes for the human editor
- I replaced the place name and the local cheer with decodable language to meet programme rules. If a cultural reference is required, consider adding a glossary panel outside the decodable text.
- I used “phone” to cover the new grapheme ph. The handset is not visible in the image; if that feels off, you could instead show Grandad holding a phone on that page, or swap “phone” for another ph word shown in art.
- I avoided all -ed past forms to respect the stated rule. If your scope does permit regular -ed at L5, the text could be shifted into past tense quite easily.
- I avoided soft c/soft g throughout, as those correspondences are not in the cumulative list.
- Title spirit “Reading Together” is kept through shared instructions and dialogue; if you want an even stronger “reading” angle, we could add a small sign or card with a printed instruction in the art on Pages 2–3.
