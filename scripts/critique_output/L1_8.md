### 2. Narrative critique
- The story idea is clear: child, yak, a box of figs, yak sits on the box, box is moved out of reach, they share a fig. The sequence makes sense and the images generally match.
- However, several lines break decodability (“have”, “Oh”, “of”, “munch”), which will stop an L1.8 reader.
- Some pages are wordy, and a few phrases feel like adult summary rather than child‑friendly spoken English.
- Image–text slips: Page 2 says “I zip it up” while the lid is visibly open; Page 3 says “sat on the box” though the yak has a hoof on it (still workable); Page 4 uses “on top of the hut” but is long and includes “of”.
- Emotional arc is there (surprise/problem/solution/sharing), but it could be told with shorter, natural sentences that keep the focus sounds.

### 3. Level appropriateness
- Current text often exceeds the “1 sentence of 3–5 words per page” requirement.
- It uses many taught graphemes, and it does include the focus x/y/z (six, yak, zip), but the non‑decodable words undermine accessibility.
- Words/book target (40–80) clashes with the single‑sentence, 6‑page format. With 6 pages × 3–5 words, the max is ~30 words. We should prioritise the per‑page rule; see editor note below.

### 4. Proposed rewrite
One sentence per page, 3–5 words, fully decodable with L1.8 graphemes and tricky words. Keeps the plot and matches images.

Page 1
- I am with a yak.

Page 2
- I zip the box.

Page 3
- The yak sat on it.

Page 4
- I pick the box up.

Page 5
- It is on the hut.

Page 6
- The yak gets six figs.

### 5. Notes for the human editor
- Word count: With the “1 sentence per page” constraint, this rewrite totals 29 words. That cannot reach the 40–80 target; consider allowing 2 short sentences on 2–3 pages if the higher word count is essential.
- Page 2 art: The line “I zip the box” assumes the child is mid‑zip. If possible, show a clear zip action on the lid to tighten alignment.
- Page 3 art: The yak appears to press a hoof onto the box rather than fully sitting. “Sat on it” still reads naturally; alternatively “The yak is on it” would match more literally but is less event‑like.
- I avoided “of”, “have”, and “munch” for decodability. “can not” was also avoided to keep lines short, but could be reintroduced on Page 5 if you prefer: “The yak can not get it.” (6 words — beyond limit).
- Chosen verbs (“pick … up”, “gets”) are everyday British English and fully decodable at this stage, while also giving y/x/z coverage via yak/six/zip.
