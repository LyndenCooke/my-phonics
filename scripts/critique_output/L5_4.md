### 2. Narrative critique
- The emotional arc (lonely child at a celebration, then welcomed) is clear in text, but several scenes do not match the images:
  - Images show a sunny market street; the text mentions celebration, drums and fire that are not in the pictures.
  - Page 5 names “acarajé”, which is not pictured; stalls show mixed fruit.
  - Pages 7–8 describe dancing; images show walking and then a hug with Dad.
- Repetition such as “But not me” supports the feeling but also pads the text.
- Overall, the story can be told from the text alone, but image–text misalignment weakens comprehension.

### 3. Level appropriateness
- Large number of non‑decodable words makes the current text unsuitable for L5.4.
- Sentence length is generally shorter than the brief (often 3–7 words).
- Words/book are below target.
- Focus graphemes ore/oor/ire/ear/ure/tion and new digraphs ph/kn/wr are scarcely used; the piece leans on untaught spellings instead of building from earlier levels.

### 4. Proposed rewrite
Goal: keep the market‑day feel and the “reading together” spirit of welcoming, while strictly using the cumulative code and foregrounding ore, oor, ire, ear, ure, tion, plus ph/kn/wr. Four–five sentences per page, 8–13 words each. British English.

Page 1
- Dad and I came to a bright market street.  
- Doors stood open, with flowers over rails on each floor.  
- Dad went to a stall, and I sat near a door.  
- I did not know anyone and felt a bit left out.

Page 2
- I could hear chat and calls from each stall and door.  
- I went the wrong way and slid by the pears.  
- Men and mums looked at them, but no one saw me.  
- I felt my fear rise and I went back.

Page 3
- I sat on a step by a tall brown door.  
- People were near, but no one came to talk.  
- I shut my eyes and counted to ten, very slow.  
- I did not want to cry, but I was tired.

Page 4
- Then a boy came by and sat down near me.  
- He gave me a big grin and held out his hand.  
- “Do you want to look at that stall?” he said.  
- I went with him, and my fear went away.

Page 5
- We met a man at a stall of pears and melons.  
- He let us hold a melon, and it was big.  
- My new pal put it on top like a hat.  
- I chose a ripe pear, and we paid the man.  
- He gave us a small portion to share at once.

Page 6
- We sat on the steps and ate the pear and melon.  
- Sweet, wet bits ran on my chin and on my shirt.  
- We both smiled more and more, with pure joy inside.  
- When we were done, we slid the peels in a bin.

Page 7
- Then my pal said, “Look, I know where Dad is.”  
- We went down the street, and he ran before me.  
- We got one more pear and a wrap for the rest.  
- I said, “Thank you,” to him, and he said, “See you soon.”

Page 8
- Dad knelt and held me, and I felt safe again.  
- He got a photo of us with my new pal.  
- I said, “Can I see him once more this year?”  
- Dad said yes, and I waved to the boy by the door.  
- This street felt like home now, and my heart was light.

Focus sound coverage in rewrite
- ore: before, more;  
- oor: door;  
- ire: tired;  
- ear: near, hear, pear, tear (implicit in “pear”), year;  
- ure: pure;  
- tion: portion;  
- ph: photo;  
- kn: know, knelt;  
- wr: wrong, wrap.

### 5. Notes for the human editor
- I avoided “together”, “music”, “dance/dancing”, “colour/colourful”, and “children” as they are not decodable with this code-set.  
- I used “melon” and “pear” so the fruit matches plausibly with the art while staying decodable; “mango/banana/orange/fruit/juice” were avoided for code reasons.  
- “peels” appears on the steps in the art; if that seems off, swap to “rinds”, but “rind(s)” uses “ind” which is fine, though the image shows banana-like peels.  
- “Thank you” uses tricky “you”; if you prefer no direct speech marks, it can be set as narrative.  
- If “alone” is acceptable in your house style, it could be reintroduced; I kept it out to stay conservative.  
- If the programme prefers proper names, choose ones fully decodable (e.g., Ben, Sam).
