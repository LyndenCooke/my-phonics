### 2. Narrative critique
- The story arc is strong and clear: May rushes ahead; Tom goes slower and notices more; they share a special wildlife moment; May learns to value Tom’s method. Text alone carries the plot.
- Image–text alignment is mostly good, but “lyrebird” and “Aboriginal dot art” require graphemes we can’t use. Also “visitor centre/gallery” wording vs our allowed code set is risky.
- Lexical density of -ous/-ible/-able words is excellent for Level 6, though at times it edges toward word-drill (e.g., “scrumptious … edible … delicious” stacked). Still serviceable.
- Past tense with many -ed forms is consistent, but our rules only explicitly permit -s. This needs a policy call or a tense change.
- A few lines are long/complex and may exceed breath units for emerging independent readers.

### 3. Level appropriateness
- Sentences are often long and fit L6 expectations, but some are over 20 words. Others are very short. Consistency could improve.
- Word count appears within the 380–500 target.
- It makes strong use of prior code plus the new focus endings ous/cious/tious/able/ible.
- Several non-decodable proper nouns/terms (“Mia,” “lyrebird,” “centre,” “Aboriginal,” “journal,” “eucalyptus”) violate our strict decodability rule and must be replaced.

### 4. Proposed rewrite
Notes on this rewrite:
- British English maintained.
- Girl’s name changed to May (fully decodable).
- “Lyrebird” replaced with “fan tail mimic bird” to match images without breaking code.
- “Visitor centre/gallery” → “visitor hall” and “First Nations dot art.”
- Past tense minimised; sentences kept to 10–15 words each; 5–6 sentences per page.

Page 1
- May feels ambitious at the start of the bush walk trail today.
- The enormous Blue Mountains stretch before them, with famous blue haze above the peaks.
- She says, “I will spot it all first today, as I am the most capable.”
- Tom says nothing, opens his small notebook, and starts a careful sketch.
- Dad waits nearby with water, smiling at the ambitious start to their day.

Page 2
- The trail leads them through spacious groves of tall gum trees and ferns.
- May strides ahead, ambitious and keen, pointing at everything that seems glorious or enormous.
- “Look at that gorgeous parrot in the branches,” she calls to Tom.
- She adds, “Those enormous cliffs are visible from here, and this walk is famous.”
- Tom walks behind, slower and more cautious, with his notebook open in hand.
- He crouches by a fallen log, peering at something small and precious.

Page 3
- “Tom, you are too cautious and too slow,” May says with a frown.
- “If you keep stopping, it will be impossible to reach the lookout.”
- She is not gracious about it, and she stamps off down the track.
- Tom shuts his notebook and tries to keep up, but feels nervous.
- From deep in the bush he hears something incredible, a curious mix of calls.
- He stops, holds his breath, and listens while the sound rises and falls.

Page 4
- Tom steps off the trail, cautious and sensible, placing each foot with care.
- Behind a thick fern, he sights a wondrous fan tail mimic bird.
- Its enormous tail feathers lift and fan like a gorgeous silver sail.
- The bird performs a famous song, copying calls from many creatures around it.
- It is the most incredible thing Tom has ever been able to see.
- He holds his breath and sketches, fast but careful, in his notebook.

Page 5
- “May,” Tom whispers, “come here, be sensible, and move very slowly to me.”
- May pads back along the trail, still a little suspicious of this fuss.
- She crouches beside Tom, and her face changes when she sees the bird.
- She breathes, “That is remarkable, and I had no idea it was here.”
- She grows conscious that she rushed past something precious in her haste.
- She realises speed is not admirable if it makes her miss something valuable.

Page 6
- When the mimic bird slips back into the bush, they walk on together.
- This time May lets Tom set the pace, which feels calm and sensible.
- He points out observable things she never noticed, like a notable pattern in bark.
- He finds a tiny bush berry that is edible and delicious.
- They breathe in the delicious smell of gum leaves warming in the sun.
- “You are admirable at this,” May says, and Tom blushes but smiles.

Page 7
- Near the end of the trail they reach the visitor hall by the car park.
- On the walls hangs a display of First Nations dot art, swirling and joyous.
- Tom holds up his notebook and compares his sketches with the paintings.
- “Your sketches are incredible,” says Dad, who was comfortable walking at the back.
- “You were responsible and sensible, and you saw more than anyone.”
- May nods, not adventurous in the same way, yet proud of his remarkable skill.

Page 8
- That evening, Tom reads aloud from his notebook while May sits beside him.
- He reads, “Today was an incredible bush walk, with enormous mountains and blue haze.”
- He reads, “I was cautious and still, and I spotted a precious mimic bird performing.”
- He reads, “May missed it at first, but we went back and watched together.”
- He reads, “It was the most glorious and wondrous moment of the whole walk.”
- He closes the notebook, and May smiles and says, “You are remarkable, Tom.”

### 5. Notes for the human editor
- I changed the girl’s name from Mia to May to keep absolute decodability. If you must keep Mia, we would need to whitelist it as a tricky proper noun.
- “Lyrebird” and “veil” were replaced; the images still work with “fan tail mimic bird.”
- “Aboriginal dot art” became “First Nations dot art” to maintain respect and decodability; please confirm cultural style guide preference.
- I avoided “centre” and “gallery” by using “visitor hall.” If “centre” must stay, it should be added to the tricky list.
- Several words with final y = /ee/ (tiny, berry, many) remain. If that mapping is not considered taught at this level, swap “tiny” → “small,” “berry” → “bush fruit” (but “fruit” needs ui), or approve final‑y=/ee/.
- Past-tense -ed is used sparingly in the rewrite. If -ed is not approved at L6, change to present (e.g., “May misses it at first”). If it is approved, we can re-introduce more natural past where desired.
- I kept strong coverage of the new focus endings across pages, but avoided forced, drill-like phrasing.
